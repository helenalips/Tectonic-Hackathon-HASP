SD Worx Hackathon Challenge: Unlock the Knowledge Within

Tagline: Find it. Understand it. Trust it.

Core question:

"How might we turn fragmented organisational knowledge into a trusted shared resource?"

1. The challenge in one paragraph

Large organisations hold a lot of knowledge, but it only creates value when people can use it with confidence. Too often it is scattered, duplicated, outdated or locked away in systems, teams and individual experience. The task is to build a focused proof of concept (PoC) that helps employees find, understand and trust the knowledge they need.

2. About SD Worx (the challenge owner)
European provider of HR, payroll and workforce operations: keeping people employed, paid and supported
More than 80 years of history
10,000+ employees, 100,000+ customers, 6M+ payslips
Operations across Europe, payroll reach in 100+ countries
Why it matters here: that scale creates a huge amount of expertise, and it also makes knowledge harder to navigate. Many answers depend on country, customer and context.
3. The problem

Knowledge at SD Worx is spread across:

Documents: policies, manuals, procedures, checklists, analyses, shared files
Communication: emails, chats, Teams channels
Systems: workflows, business applications, operational data
People: tacit knowledge that lives only in the heads of experts and experienced colleagues

Key insight: finding information is only the first step. A search can return ten answers, and an AI assistant can summarise them. The harder question is whether an answer is reliable, current and relevant to this customer, country or situation.

It is fundamentally a trust problem. Uncertainty slows decisions, creates repeated work and makes expertise hard to reuse. A useful solution shows:

the answer
why it deserves confidence
where uncertainty remains
who can help when documents are not enough
4. Example friction scenarios (from the brief)

Scenario A: urgent customer question

An AI assistant finds 3 documents: one recently updated, one without an owner, and one that may apply to another country
A colleague shares contradictory information from a Teams conversation
Result: the employee has found information but still cannot act with confidence

Scenario B: portfolio handover

A payroll consultant inherits a client portfolio
In the past, the handover was one document and a one-hour conversation
Today the knowledge is spread across documents, chats, workflows, applications, business data and experts in different teams

"The information exists. Confidence does not come automatically."

5. What to build
A focused PoC that makes organisational knowledge easier to find, trust or share
Pick one meaningful problem. You do not need to solve everything.
Focus on one role, one workflow, one knowledge source or one trust signal
Make the moment of doubt tangible, then show how the PoC moves someone from:
"I found something" → "I understand why I can rely on it."

Guiding questions:

What is reliable?
What is current?
What applies in this context?
Where are the gaps?
Who has relevant expertise?
Which answer should a person trust?
6. Inspiration areas (inspiration, not a checklist)
Area	Question
Trust	How might people recognise whether information is relevant and reliable?
Capture	How might valuable knowledge become accessible beyond inboxes, documents and siloed teams?
Detect	How might conflicting, duplicated, missing or outdated knowledge become visible?
Connect	How might people find the right expertise when documents are not enough?
7. Design principles and what the jury likely values
Do not start from prescribed technology. Start from the moment of doubt, friction or uncertainty you want to change.
No black boxes. Make trust visible, explainable and useful. Don't hide complexity; surface it.
Combine technical ingenuity with a deep understanding of human behaviour
Be curious and challenge assumptions
Narrow scope with real potential beats broad and shallow
Build something bold
8. Possible trust signals to consider (derived from the brief)
Recency: when was it last updated?
Ownership: is there a known, accountable owner?
Scope or applicability: which country, customer, product or situation does it apply to?
Consistency: does it conflict with other sources, including chats?
Source type: official policy, informal chat or expert opinion?
Coverage gaps: what is missing or uncertain?
Human fallback: who is the expert to ask?
